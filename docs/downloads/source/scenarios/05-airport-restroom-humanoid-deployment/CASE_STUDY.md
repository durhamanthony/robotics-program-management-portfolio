# Case 05 — Airport Restroom Humanoid Cleaning Pilot

> **Evidence-confidence key:** [PB-H] public benchmark or authoritative source/high confidence; [RBE-M] range-based estimate/medium confidence; [SA-L] scenario assumption/low confidence; [DC-L] disclosed derived calculation/confidence inherits the weakest input; [UPV] unverified production value/block commitment until approved.


## Scenario

Pacific Gateway International Airport is a fictional large international airport. Terminal 4 operates continuously and cannot be rebuilt around a cleaning machine. The airport authorized a controlled 90-day pilot using two humanoid robots in four existing public restrooms. The rooms retain their current doors, stalls, toilets, urinals, sinks, mirrors, dispensers, waste bins, and human-accessible clearances.

These simulations are fictitious generic scenarios for demonstration purposes only.

## Why a humanoid form factor is being tested

The pilot asks whether a human-scale machine can work within rooms built for people: pass through existing doors, reach fixtures at human height, maneuver around partitions, use hand tools, replenish dispensers, and return to a charger without permanent construction. A wheeled floor scrubber remains appropriate for open floors but does not cover the full fixture-cleaning and replenishment workflow. The pilot does not assume that humanoid form is automatically economical; it tests that question.

## Controlled operating envelope

- Four mapped restrooms in one terminal concourse.
- Three scheduled routine-cleaning missions per restroom per day.
- The restroom is closed and entry-controlled while the robot operates.
- Routine soil only. Sharps, blood, vomit, fecal contamination outside a fixture, smoke, standing water, aggressive behavior, or an unidentified chemical trigger a safe stop and human specialist response.
- Airport custodians retain inspection, chemical selection, biohazard response, corrective cleaning, supply custody, and reopening authority.
- No electrical work, plumbing repair, autonomous public interaction, facial recognition, or unsupervised operation around passengers.

## Three coordinated project-manager viewpoints

### Seller, system integrator, and implementer

The seller Project Manager owns the customer contract, integrated deployment plan, interface control, site survey, configuration, commissioning, training, acceptance evidence, warranty handoff, and issue resolution across the airport and manufacturer.

### Humanoid manufacturer

The manufacturer Project Manager owns the pilot product baseline, hardware and software configuration, verification evidence, cleaning-tool interface, chemical/material compatibility, reliability growth, spares, release records, and defect correction. Approval is limited to the defined pilot configuration; it is not a general product release.

### Airport buyer and operator

The airport Project Manager owns procurement, operational need, labor and service baseline, stakeholder authority, terminal access, cybersecurity and privacy approval, custodial procedures, passenger protection, union and workforce engagement, daily operating controls, acceptance, benefits measurement, and the expansion decision.

## Completed outcome

The two robots completed 1,048 of 1,080 scheduled routine missions. A stratified sample of 270 completed missions received a twelve-point inspection; 248 passed on the first inspection. All 60 witnessed safety and recovery tests passed. Every simulated sharps, body-fluid, door-entry, network-loss, and chemical exception entered a safe state and routed to an authorized human.

The pilot was accepted as a controlled operational learning result. Expansion was not authorized. The five-year planning Total Cost of Ownership was $3,134,500, while the measured routine-task capacity released by this four-restroom pilot annualized to only $68,985 at the airport's fictional loaded labor rate. The airport required binding quotes, a revised multi-restroom operating design, and measured non-labor service value before any scale decision.

## Phase 2 recommendation developed after closeout

The program team preserved the pilot result and designed a different scale case rather than changing historical numbers. The proposed Phase 2 covers 12 restrooms with two robots under Robotics-as-a-Service (RaaS). Eight 10-minute short visits and two 30-minute deep cleans per room per day create 28 productive fleet-hours per day. The robots are staggered; neither is described as operating continuously.

At a planning subscription of USD 6,000 per robot per month, the five-year Total Cost of Ownership is USD 1,270,000 versus USD 1,609,650 of task-capacity value. Planning net benefit is USD 339,650, Return on Investment (ROI) is 26.7%, and simple payback on initial integration is approximately 18 months. Phase 2 is Conditional Green only if a time study validates the workload and the final subscription remains at or below USD 6,595 per robot per month.

## Project closeout decision

1. Accept the seller's contracted pilot deliverables and close implementation punch-list items.
2. Accept the manufacturer's pilot baseline and verification record, but do not authorize General Availability.
3. Transfer the four-restroom operating runbook, exception process, inspection records, spare kit, and support contacts to airport operations.
4. Continue no more than 60 days under existing controls for data collection; do not add restrooms, shifts, chemicals, or tasks without change review.
5. Reopen the investment decision only when vendor quotations and an observed airport workload model demonstrate a defensible service or financial benefit.
